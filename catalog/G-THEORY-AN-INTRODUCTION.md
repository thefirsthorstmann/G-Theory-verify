## 1 · The state of things

Down through the ages there have been innumerable scientific and mathematical theories, ideas, descriptions, experiments and models. From this plethora two generally dominant spaces have formed: classical physics, as exemplified by General Relativity, and quantum physics, as expressed by the Standard Model. Both approaches have displayed, over the years, an incredible variety of extraordinarily original conceptual, physical and mathematical ideas, and many a brilliant mind has poured the whole of their life into their development. The issue as it stands now is that these two theories, in spite of a great deal of experimental confirmation of both, remain incompatible with each other.

As a result it has become *de rigueur* to propose what are called "theories of everything," which seek to unify these two opposing areas. There has come to be such a multitude of these that it is impossible even to keep track of them. All have proponents, many of whom are nothing less than full-fledged experts from the storied halls of institutions such as Oxford, Cambridge, Yale, Harvard, MIT and many more around the world — all educated to the Nth degree, and agreeing on very little. Then there are the others, with no such education but brilliant and thrifty nonetheless, who manage to invent all sorts of innovative approaches in their garages and shops just the same.

Philosophically and spiritually there is similar diversity. Just as there are true believers in almighty God, there are minds who reject any notion of religion or creation as stone-age myth — attributed to ancient humans being scared of the dark, or needing to placate deities they could not understand as scientific phenomena — and all points in between. There is panpsychism, animism, materialism, rationalism, naturalism, and many other isms too numerous to mention. Arguments and feuds abound: a rich tapestry of stories, with heroes and villains engaged in phenomenal leaps of mind to the heights of originality, concurrent with just as phenomenal expeditions into the depths of political intrigue and plagiarism.

It is such that the story of science these days reads more like an ancient epic poem than a college textbook. Like the legends, it is a space replete with strange symbols bearing obscure meanings, covering blackboards and computer screens as if they were old treasure maps, or the walls of primordial caverns adorned with glyphs written in red ochre. And indeed — are we actually different now from those people? Do we understand our symbols any better, or even as well, as they did theirs?

And so, in all this great diversity, just about the only thing there isn't is agreement.

Perhaps some new rules are in order regarding what can be considered a theory of everything — since it is clear that there can exist only one overall empirical basis. Let us make a rough outline of the guidelines to which any such theory should be held.

## 2 · Theories of everything — a rough guide

**1. Unitary explanation.** Phenomenally, it must all be under one roof: a plausible means of unifying the forces, and at least a posited basis for the mechanics of gravity, electromagnetism, consciousness, and the logic of their unification.

**2. A corresponding unitary expression** — in a sense, *a symbol* — that is on the one hand exactly that, symbolic, and on the other contains the means of unpacking, in a technical sense, the basis of the theory. To understand this it is better to look to the ancient world, and to symbols like the Ouroboros, the Hopi symbol of Life, or the Egyptian Ankh: to the peoples who gave us these symbols, each represented a complete understanding, held in one figure. What is called for is a modern equivalent: translatable into decimal mathematics as a fully accurate, self-contained, non-allegorical geometric description that is all-inclusive — that is to say, complete.

**3. Origin.** While assigning a motivation for the existence of the universe is perhaps the greatest mystery of all, at the very least there should be a plausible and logical process — a procedural form — for how the universe unfolded.

**4. A real and calculable structural definition of fields**, logical and straightforward. The standard account computes with fields to extraordinary accuracy while leaving their ontology open: virtual processes serve as calculational devices, renormalization absorbs the infinities the formalism generates, and the question of what a field *is* stands officially unanswered. A theory of everything does not inherit that license. It must say what its fields are, in terms that are structural, calculable, and falsifiable.

**5. Gravity and electromagnetism.** The relationship of the strong, weak and electromagnetic forces, though not perfect, is functional. The relationship of gravity to electromagnetism is not. These must be connected — directly, logically, and verifiably.

**6. Quantization.** Everything, without exception, must be expressible in this manner. No averages, no straight lines, and no curves either. Quanta: points with values. Stated technically: the continuum may serve as an instrument of calculation, but it cannot be admitted as a physical object — a physical process is a finite count of definite states, and any description requiring infinitely many of them in finite time has left physics for idealization. The same discipline settles the ends of the number line: zero and infinity enter as the markers of a bounded system — bounds approached, never attained or occupied — and never as quantities a process reaches. A completed infinity, an attained zero, a smooth curve traversing uncountably many positions: each is the same object in different dress, and a theory of everything must be able to say of each either *this is a count* or *this is an instrument*, with nothing left over. Averages, limits and envelopes remain welcome as instruments; the claim is only that what exists and acts is the count beneath them.

**7. Harmonic structure.** The mode arithmetic of bounded vibration — whole-number ratios, overtones, commas, the mechanics every string and column of air obeys — is mathematics realized physically. It is also the one such domain to which human perception has direct access: a listener verifies an octave without an instrument. The criterion is not that a theory mention music. It is that the theory state, and defend, what role harmonic structure plays in its foundations — whether the recurrence of the same small integers in vibration, in atomic structure, and in the dimensionless constants is structural or coincidental. A theory that cannot say is incomplete on a question physics itself raises, since the same ratios appear wherever its own equations are bounded.

**8. Common sense about tools.** Every description commits to a mathematical instrument, and the instrument shapes what the description can say. A theory of everything should justify its choice of tools domain by domain, rather than inheriting them by default. Complication that grows with the formalism rather than with the phenomenon is a warning sign that the tool, not the world, is being described. No claim is made here that any one instrument is best for any one domain; which mathematics fits perception, for example, is an open empirical question. The working choice of this series — arithmetic and ratio — is exactly that, a working choice, and it is to be judged by what the papers derive with it, not by assertion.

## 3 · Present tense

Which brings us to the work you are about to read, and the heavy limitations I have placed upon its use of mathematics. Understanding the rationale for this limitation starts with a question that is simple to formulate and difficult to answer definitively: **can our physical universe, and the processes within it, be described more simply?** Can a Higgs boson be described with no algebraic functions? Fields with no tensor calculus? Harmonic structures with no Hilbert spaces? Wave collapse? Electromagnetism? Gravity?

Right away, many would say: *"that's impossible!"*

Conversely, it is completely fair to ask whether it really is impossible — or whether we merely think it is, because we haven't figured out how to do it yet.

As is said, the definition of insanity is doing the same thing while expecting different results — and this forms the basis of the counter-argument. Can we really say it is safe to assume that the means by which we have created this apparent mess are the same means by which we will resolve it? Is it sound logic to think that ever more complex equations and descriptions will somehow result in clearer, simpler explanations? Are we truly using all of the tools — or even the right tools? Is it possible to formulate technical definitions that are both simpler *and* more precise?

The papers in this catalog are one sustained attempt at an answer. They are held to the eight criteria above, and to a further set of standards and practices this introduction only names: every claim in them is graded for exactly what it is, every number is verified by machine before it is asserted, and every live claim carries the stated conditions under which it would be falsified. The reader is never asked to believe; the reader is asked to check.

Since this introduction first posted, the series' account of gravitation has been published in full — the fifth criterion's connection stated and derived, with seventeen conditions of refutation and a public claim map (row 12 of the catalog). The registered falsification schedule has reached its second version, and the whole suite — over a thousand public assertions — re-derives on any reader's machine from github.com/thefirsthorstmann/g-theory-verify.
